# The Rhythm of Your Heart

## A Novel

### By Walusimbi Leon (SGSS)

---

## Part One: London

### Chapter 1: The Gallery on Brick Lane

The silence was never empty.

Soo-jin Park stood before her latest canvas in the Whitechapel Gallery, watching the crowd move around her work like water around a stone. The painting was large — eight feet by six — a storm of color and texture that depicted sound as she imagined it: waves of deep indigo crashing against shores of gold, ripples of crimson radiating outward from a central point of absolute white. She had titled it "What I Hear When You Speak," and it had taken her four months and seventeen attempts to get right.

She was deaf. Had been since birth. The world had tried to explain sound to her a thousand ways — vibrations, frequencies, air pressure, the movement of tiny bones in the ear she could not feel. But none of it mattered, because her world was not defined by what she could not hear. It was defined by what she could see, what she could feel, what she could taste and touch and know in the deep, wordless way that bypasses the ears entirely.

She saw music everywhere. In the way people walked — the staccato of a businessman late for a meeting, the legato of a couple strolling hand in hand. In the way rain fell — the quick tap-tap-tap on a windowpane, the slow roll of thunder across the sky. In the way hands moved when they spoke — the sharp chop of emphasis, the gentle wave of explanation, the trembling of someone trying not to cry.

Her mother had told her once: "The world is full of music, Soo-jin. You just hear it differently."

Her mother had been right. But her mother was in Seoul, six thousand miles away, and Soo-jin was here, in London, standing in front of a painting that was really a love letter to a language she had never heard.

The gallery opening was in full swing. Wine glasses clinked. Conversations buzzed. She watched the mouths of strangers forming words she could not hear but could read with the fluency of a native speaker — lips shaping vowels, teeth clicking against consonants, the subtle dance of jaw and tongue that turned breath into meaning. She had learned English by watching. Korean by feeling. Japanese by reading the space between gestures.

A woman approached — the gallery curator, elegant in black, her silver hair swept back in a chignon. She signed to Soo-jin in the British Sign Language she had learned specifically for this exhibition.

*Your work is extraordinary. The critics are speechless.*

Soo-jin smiled. *That's ironic. Critics are never speechless.*

The curator laughed. *There's someone I want you to meet. A musician. He's been staring at your central piece for ten minutes.*

Soo-jin followed the curator's gaze across the room. A man stood alone before her largest painting — the one she called "Forty-Seven Seconds," a depiction of the exact moment she had fallen in love for the first time, rendered in abstract color and motion. He was tall, lean, with dark skin and close-cropped hair, wearing a suit that didn't quite fit, as though he had bought it specifically for tonight and hadn't had time to get it tailored. His posture was wrong for a gallery visitor — too loose, too easy, the posture of someone who spent his life on stages, not in hushed rooms full of art.

He was reading her artist's statement. She watched his eyes move across the words, watched his lips move slightly as he read — a habit she had noticed in many people, the invisible subvocalization that turned reading into a kind of silent speech.

She walked toward him. Her heels made no sound on the polished concrete floor, but he looked up as she approached, startled, as though he had sensed her there without hearing her.

"You're the artist," he said. Not a question.

She nodded.

"This painting." He gestured at "Forty-Seven Seconds." "What is it about?"

She signed, then spoke — her voice careful, measured, shaped by years of speech therapy and a determination that bordered on defiance. "It is about a moment. Forty-seven seconds. The exact length of time between when I realized I was in love and when I ran away."

He stared at her. "You can count the seconds of falling in love?"

"I can count everything. I just can't hear."

A long pause. She watched his face cycle through the emotions she had come to recognize — surprise, pity, correction, curiosity. Most people stopped at pity. This man went all the way to curiosity.

"I'm Marcus," he said. "Marcus Cole."

"Soo-jin."

"You're Korean."

"Half. My mother is Korean. My father was American."

"Was?"

"He died. Three years ago. In Seoul."

Another pause. She watched him process this — the loss, the distance, the careful way she had constructed the sentence to contain the grief without releasing it.

"I'm sorry," he said. And meant it. She could tell by the way his eyes softened, the way his shoulders dropped a fraction of an inch, the way his hands — long-fingered, the hands of a pianist — went still at his sides.

"What do you do, Marcus Cole?" she asked.

"I play piano."

"Professionally?"

"Unfortunately." He smiled, and it transformed his face — made it younger, warmer, the kind of smile that made you want to smile back. "I'm performing at the Barbican tomorrow night. Jazz trio. You should come."

"I don't hear music," she said.

"I know." He looked at her painting — the storm of color, the waves of sound made visible. "But you see it. That's better."

### Chapter 2: Forty-Seven Seconds

She went to the concert.

She sat in the third row, close enough to feel the vibration of the piano through the floor, through the seat, through the bones of her spine. She watched Marcus's hands — and understood.

His fingers moved like dancers. Left hand steady, rhythmic, grounding — the bass notes, the foundation, the heartbeat. Right hand free, improvisational, soaring — the melody, the story, the thing that made people close their eyes and forget where they were. Together they created something she could feel in her teeth, in her ribs, in the hollow space behind her sternum where silence lived.

She watched the other musicians too — the bassist, the drummer — and saw how they communicated without words. A glance. A nod. A shift in weight. The invisible language of ensemble playing, where each musician listened to the others not with ears but with bodies, with instincts, with the deep collective knowledge that comes from playing together so many times that the music becomes a single organism, breathing and moving as one.

She understood that language. It was the language of her paintings — the way color spoke to color, the way texture answered texture, the way a single brushstroke could change the meaning of everything around it. Music and painting were not opposites. They were translations. Different dialects of the same mother tongue.

When the set ended, she didn't applaud — she couldn't — but she stood, and she raised her hands above her head, and she signed: *Beautiful.*

Marcus saw her from the stage. He saw her hands moving in the silence that was her world, and he smiled — not the stage smile, not the performer's smile, but the real one, the one she had seen in the gallery, the one that made her chest ache with something she could not name.

After the show, he found her in the lobby. "You came."

"You invited me."

"And?"

She searched for the right words. English failed her sometimes — it was a language built for sound, full of words that meant things she could not experience. How do you describe music to someone who has never heard it? How do you describe silence to someone who has never known it?

"I saw your hands," she said. "And I understood."

He waited.

"Your left hand is the earth. Steady. Constant. The place everything grows from. Your right hand is the sky. Free. Wild. Going wherever it wants. And the space between them —" She paused, her hands shaping the concept in sign language even as her voice struggled to find the English. "The space between them is where the music lives."

Marcus stared at her. "In fifteen years of performing," he said slowly, "no one has ever described my playing that way."

"Maybe no one was listening."

He laughed — a real laugh, deep and sudden, the kind that comes from being genuinely surprised. "Can I buy you dinner?"

"It's almost midnight."

"Dinner, then. Late-night food. The kind of food you eat when you should have gone home hours ago."

She smiled. "Okay."

---

They walked through the empty streets of Whitechapel, the city quiet around them, the only sounds the distant hum of traffic and the occasional burst of laughter from a pub still open. Marcus talked with his hands — she noticed that immediately. Not sign language, but the expressive, sweeping gestures of a musician, a man who had spent his life translating feeling into movement. She read his hands like she read faces — fluently, instinctively, catching every nuance.

He told her about New Orleans. About growing up in the Tremé neighborhood, the oldest African-American neighborhood in the country, where music poured from every doorway and the streets themselves seemed to hum. About his grandmother, who had played gospel piano in a church on Bayou Road for forty years. About the night she died — how the church had filled with musicians who played through the night, how the music had gone on and on and on, as though they could hold her soul in the sound and keep it from leaving.

"She was the one who taught me," Marcus said. "Not to play. To listen. She said music isn't in the notes. It's in the silence between the notes. The pause. The breath. The space where the listener fills in what they feel."

Soo-jin stopped walking. She turned to face him, and her eyes were bright with something that might have been recognition or might have been grief.

"That is what I paint," she said. "The space between."

He looked at her — really looked at her — and she saw something shift in his face. Not pity. Not curiosity. Something deeper. Something that recognized her across the silence that separated their worlds.

"Then we speak the same language," he said.

"We just use different alphabets."

### Chapter 3: The Language of Hands

They fell into a rhythm. Tuesday evenings at the gallery. Thursday nights at his rehearsals. Saturday mornings walking through Borough Market, where she pointed out colors and textures and he described sounds and rhythms, each translating their world for the other in an act of daily, deliberate intimacy.

She taught him to sign. Basic things at first — hello, thank you, beautiful, music. He was terrible at it, his long pianist's fingers clumsy with the precise geometry of sign language, but he tried with a seriousness that moved her.

"The handshape for 'music' is this?" he asked, forming the sign imperfectly.

She corrected his fingers gently. "Like this. Your hands are making the shape of sound waves. Moving outward from the heart."

"And 'love'?"

She showed him. Her hands crossed over her chest, holding something invisible, something precious.

"That's it?" he said. "Love is just holding something close?"

"Love is protecting something fragile," she said. "Something that can break if you hold it too tightly or too loosely."

He practiced the sign over and over, his face concentrated, his lips moving silently. She watched his hands learn the shape of a word he had never needed to see before, and something inside her opened — a door she had kept locked, a room she had not entered since her father died.

She told him about Seoul. About growing up between worlds — Korean at home, American at school, deaf in both. About the way her mother had insisted she learn to speak, had taken her to speech therapy three times a week, had celebrated every new word like a birthday. About the way her father, an American teacher who had come to Korea on a Fulbright and stayed for love, had learned Korean Sign Language so he could talk to his daughter without barriers.

"He said I taught him the most important lesson of his life," Soo-jin told Marcus one evening, as they sat on the Millennium Bridge watching the Thames flow beneath them. "He said I taught him that listening has nothing to do with ears."

"What did it have to do with?"

"Attention. Presence. The willingness to be still and watch and wait for the meaning to arrive."

Marcus was quiet for a long time. Then he said: "My grandmother said something similar. She said music is just attention. You put your attention on the silence, and the silence gives you a song."

"Your grandmother sounds like she was wise."

"She was a church piano player from New Orleans. They're all wise. It's a job requirement."

Soo-jin laughed — silently, her shoulders shaking, her hands covering her mouth. He had learned to recognize her laugh by the way her body moved, the way her eyes crinkled at the corners, the way her whole face became a kind of music.

"Tell me something," he said. "When you paint — when you're in the middle of it, brush moving, colors mixing — what does it feel like?"

She thought for a long time. "It feels like the way I imagine music feels. Like there is a rhythm underneath everything, and I am following it. Not leading. Following. The painting tells me where to go, the same way a melody tells a musician where to go. I am not the creator. I am the translator."

"That's exactly how I feel when I play."

"I know. I saw it. The first night, at the Barbican. Your hands were not making the music. They were following it."

He looked at her, and the air between them changed — became dense with something unspoken, something that lived in the space between words, in the silence that both of them understood better than sound.

"Soo-jin," he said.

"Yes?"

"I think I'm falling in love with you."

She looked at him. She did not blush — she never blushed, her skin too dark for it to show — but her hands trembled, and he saw it, and he understood.

"How many seconds?" he asked.

She smiled. "I haven't counted yet."

## Part Two: Seoul

### Chapter 4: Across the Water

Three months later, Marcus moved to Seoul.

Not for her — he told himself that, told her that, told his manager and his booking agent and his mother in New Orleans who cried on the phone for twenty minutes. He had been offered a residency at the Blue Square, one of the most prestigious jazz venues in Asia. It was an opportunity that came once in a career, and he would have been a fool to turn it down.

But he would have been a bigger fool to let Soo-jin go.

She met him at Incheon Airport, standing behind the glass barrier of the arrivals hall, her hands in the pockets of a long coat, her face unreadable. He walked toward her, dragging his suitcase, his heart hammering with a fear he had never known — the fear of loving someone in a country where you don't speak the language, where the silence is different, where everything you know is suddenly strange.

She signed as he approached: *Welcome to Korea.*

He signed back, clumsily: *Thank you. I am scared.*

She laughed — the real laugh, the one that made her whole body move. *Good. Fear means you are paying attention.*

---

Seoul was a revelation.

It was a city of contradictions — ancient palaces standing beside glass towers, Buddhist temples hidden between karaoke bars, the smell of kimchi and exhaust fumes mingling in the narrow alleys of Insadong. Soo-jin navigated it with the ease of someone who had grown up in its rhythms, pointing out the things Marcus would never have noticed — the way old women bowed to each other on the street, the way neon signs pulsed in patterns that looked like music, the way the city's noise was layered, each sound occupying its own frequency like instruments in an orchestra.

She took him to her mother's apartment in Bukchon, a traditional hanok with a courtyard garden where persimmon trees grew. Her mother, Mrs. Park, was a small, precise woman with silver-streaked hair and eyes that missed nothing. She had learned to communicate with Soo-jin through sign language and lip-reading, and she extended the same courtesy to Marcus, signing slowly, carefully, her face warm with the particular joy of a mother watching her daughter love someone who deserved it.

"She says you have kind hands," Soo-jin translated as her mother signed. "She says pianist's hands are like gardener's hands — they know how to make beautiful things grow."

"Tell her I said thank you. And that her garden is the most beautiful place I've ever seen."

Mrs. Park smiled and signed something back. Soo-jin's eyebrows rose.

"What?" Marcus asked.

"She says you should eat more. You're too thin."

Marcus laughed, and Mrs. Park laughed, and Soo-jin laughed, and for a moment the silence between them was filled with something that sounded — to Marcus, who could hear — exactly like music.

---

But Seoul also taught Marcus what silence really meant.

In New Orleans, silence was rare. The city hummed, buzzed, sang, shouted, played music from every window and doorway. Silence was the exception, not the rule. In Seoul, silence was architecture. It was built into the walls of temples, the gardens of palaces, the spaces between buildings. It was not the absence of sound but the presence of something else — a stillness, a attention, a quality of awareness that made every sound more vivid when it came.

He learned to watch Soo-jin's face the way he had learned to listen to music — with his whole body, with his attention fixed not on the surface but on the depth beneath. He learned that her eyes were her ears. That she read emotion in the angle of a jaw, the tension of a shoulder, the speed of a blink. He learned that she could tell when he was happy before he knew it himself, because his body spoke a language his mind had not yet caught up with.

And she taught him to see.

Not the way artists see — she was far beyond that. She taught him to see the way deaf people see. To notice the vibration of a passing train in the ripple of a coffee cup. To feel the bass of a nightclub in the floorboards, in the walls, in the air itself. To understand that sound was not just waves in the air but a physical presence, a force that touched everything it passed through.

"Music is not just for ears," she told him one night, as they sat in a noraebang — a karaoke room — watching the lyrics scroll across the screen while Marcus played the piano and Soo-jin felt the vibration of the keys through the bench. "Music is for bodies. For bones. For blood. The ear is just one door. There are a hundred others."

He played for her that night — not jazz, not the complex harmonies he performed on stage, but simple things. "Clair de Lune." "Moon River." "A Love Supreme." He played with his eyes closed, his hands moving across the keys with a tenderness he had never shown an audience, and she sat beside him, her hands flat on the body of the piano, feeling every note as it resonated through the wood and into her fingers.

When he finished, she was crying. He had never seen her cry.

"What is it?" he asked.

"You gave me sound," she whispered. "For the first time in my life, I heard music."

### Chapter 5: The Distance

But love, like music, cannot exist only in the moment. It must survive Tuesday mornings and Thursday evenings, the ordinary hours that fill the spaces between the extraordinary ones.

Marcus's residency ended. He had to return to New York — a recording contract, a tour, the machinery of a career that did not pause for love. Soo-jin's exhibition was touring Europe. She would be in Berlin, then Paris, then Amsterdam. Their schedules, which had aligned so perfectly in London, now diverged like parallel lines that would never meet.

"We'll make it work," Marcus said, on their last night in Seoul, sitting on the floor of her apartment, surrounded by the paintings she was packing for Europe.

"How?" she asked.

"The way we always do. We'll translate."

But translation is not the same as conversation. It is a bridge, not a home.

The video calls were harder than he expected. Not because of the language — they had developed their own pidgin of sign, speech, and gesture that worked beautifully in person but fell apart through a screen. The lag turned her signs into disconnected gestures, stripped of the flow and rhythm that gave them meaning. His voice, filtered through speakers, lost the warmth she had learned to read in his jaw, his shoulders, the way he leaned into words.

"It's not the same," she signed, her face pixelated on his laptop screen.

"I know."

"I can't read you through the screen. I can see your face, but I can't feel your presence. And without presence, the words are just shapes."

"I know."

"I miss your hands. I miss the way you talk with them. I miss the way you touch the piano keys like they're alive. I miss the way you hold your coffee cup with both hands, like it's something precious."

"I know."

"Stop saying 'I know' and come to Berlin."

"I can't. The tour —"

"Then I'll come to New York."

"Your exhibition —"

"Then we're stuck."

A long silence. The kind of silence that is not peaceful but heavy, weighted with all the things that cannot be said through a screen.

"I love you," he said.

"I know," she signed. "But love needs a body. It needs hands to hold and a shoulder to lean on and a presence that fills a room. Love is not a message. It is a verb. It requires proximity."

He closed his eyes. "I'll come to Paris after the tour. Six weeks."

"Six weeks is a long time."

"It's not forever."

"Everything is forever when you're waiting."

## Part Three: Paris

### Chapter 6: The Rhythm of Your Heart

She was in Paris when he arrived.

He found her at the Musée d'Orsay, standing before a Monet — one of the Water Lilies, massive and shimmering, the colors bleeding into each other like a dream. She didn't know he was there. He watched her from across the room, watched the way she stood — perfectly still, her head tilted slightly, her eyes moving across the canvas with the专注 of someone reading a letter from God.

He walked toward her. His footsteps made no sound on the marble floor — he had learned to walk quietly, living with her, a skill he had never known he lacked. But she turned anyway, as she always did, as though she had felt him arrive the way she felt everything — not through sound but through the displacement of air, the shift in energy, the particular quality of attention that one person directs toward another.

She saw him. Her face opened — not smiled, not laughed, but opened, like a flower turning toward the sun. And in that moment, Marcus understood what music really was.

It was not sound. It was not notes. It was not the vibration of strings or the movement of air. It was this. The look on a woman's face when she sees the person she loves after a long absence. It was the opening of a heart, the arrival of a presence, the moment when two people who have been separated by distance and time and the brutal mathematics of geography suddenly, impossibly, beautifully, find themselves in the same room again.

He opened his arms. She walked into them.

And the Monet watched, its water lilies floating in their painted silence, as two people who spoke different languages held each other in a museum in Paris and said everything that needed to be said without speaking a single word.

---

They married in Seoul, in the courtyard of her mother's hanok, under the persimmon trees. Marcus's grandmother came from New Orleans — ninety-one years old, frail but fierce, her hands still capable of playing gospel piano with a power that defied her age. She played "Amazing Grace" as Soo-jin walked down the aisle, and even though Soo-jin could not hear the notes, she could feel them — in the vibration of the wooden floor, in the resonance of the old piano's body, in the tears on the faces of the people watching.

Mrs. Park signed the wedding vows in Korean Sign Language. Marcus's grandmother signed them in English, her old hands forming the shapes with the same precision she had once used for chords. And Soo-jin and Marcus signed their vows to each other — slowly, carefully, making sure every word was shaped with love, every gesture infused with the particular tenderness of two people who had learned to communicate across the silence that separated their worlds.

"I promise to listen to you," Marcus signed, "even when there is no sound."

"I promise to see you," Soo-jin signed, "even when you are far away."

"I promise to learn your language," he signed.

"I promise to teach you mine," she signed.

And somewhere in the silence between their promises — in the space where meaning lives, where words are not needed because presence is enough — the music played. Not for ears. Not for instruments. For the two of them, and for everyone who had ever loved someone across a silence that seemed impossible to cross.

---

### Epilogue: What She Heard

Years later, when their daughter was born — a small, dark-eyed creature they named Hana, the Korean word for "one" — Marcus composed a piece for her. He called it "The Rhythm of Your Heart," and it was simple, almost primitive: a single note repeated, again and again, like a heartbeat, like a pulse, like the sound of a mother's heart heard from inside the womb.

He played it for Soo-jin in their apartment in Seoul, his hands on the piano, her hands on the body of the instrument, feeling every note as it resonated through the wood and into her fingers and up through her bones and into the place where sound became feeling, where music became meaning, where rhythm became love.

When he finished, she was crying again. He had learned not to ask why she cried. He had learned that her tears were not sadness but translation — the moment when feeling exceeded the capacity of any language, spoken or signed, and overflowed through the only channel that could contain it.

She signed to him, her hands trembling: *I heard it.*

He signed back: *What did you hear?*

She placed her hand on her chest, over her heart, and signed: *This. I heard this. The rhythm of your heart. It is the most beautiful sound in the world.*

And he understood — finally, completely, in the way that only love can teach — that the most beautiful music is not the music you hear with your ears. It is the music you feel with your heart. The rhythm of another person's love. The beat of their presence. The sound they make when they are near you, when they are holding you, when they are telling you without words that you are not alone.

The rhythm of your heart.

The only music that matters.

## About This Book

**The Rhythm of Your Heart** is a love story about a deaf artist and a jazz pianist who discover that music is not something you hear with your ears but something you feel with your heart. Set across London, Seoul, New York, and New Orleans, it is a story about the language of hands, the silence between notes, and the courage to love someone across a divide that seems impossible to cross.

It is a story for anyone who has ever communicated love without words — and for anyone who has ever believed that the most beautiful music is the one you feel rather than hear.
